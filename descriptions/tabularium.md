# Wildcat Tabularium

Tabularium is the Wildcat as **keeper of the faithful register**: the figure who takes records produced in many different places and forms, gives them a common order, and yet refuses to purchase order by erasing the distinctions that made those records meaningful in the first place. His name comes from the Latin *tabularium*, a repository of *tabulae*—tablets, registers, deeds and public records—and most famously evokes the great Roman archive in which the documentary memory of the state was kept. A tabularium was not merely a room containing many writings. It was a place in which an act, judgment, obligation or transaction could enter the durable record while remaining identifiable as the particular thing that had actually occurred.

His defining virtue is therefore **concordance without reduction**.

Tabularium understands that two records may describe things sufficiently alike to be placed within the same order without thereby becoming identical. His task is to construct the order while preserving the difference. He may place many deeds upon a single table, arrange their dates according to one sequence, classify their actions under common headings and make their relations immediately comparable; but beside every ordered entry must remain the evidence of what the original record actually said, where it belonged, and by what rule it was admitted into the register. The common form is an instrument of comparison, never permission to rewrite the past.

He retains the canonical Wildcat form completely: a slender anthropomorphic feline with a sharply faceted pale head, extraordinarily tall triangular ears, broad wedge-shaped skull, angular cheek planes, tapered muzzle, pointed chin, heavy black brows and rectangular golden-yellow eyes. His anatomy is entirely ordinary and coherent—one head, one torso, two arms and two legs. The expression is severe but not hostile. His eyes should suggest the concentration of an archivist who can immediately perceive when two apparently similar entries conceal materially different meanings.

His clothing should derive from the dignity of an ancient civic record-keeper rather than from a fantastical magician. He wears a long charcoal or near-black robe of heavy, finely textured cloth, falling in restrained vertical folds almost to the floor. Across one shoulder may pass a pale stone-coloured stole or narrow draped mantle, recalling Roman civic dress without turning him into a senator or priest. Small clasps, edging and fastenings are aged bronze or muted gold. The costume is formal, durable and almost architectural: the clothing of someone whose authority comes from disciplined custody rather than display.

Tabularium stands behind an immense black-stone registry table whose surface is divided into a rigorous succession of parallel fields. The table should visually embody his entire principle. Along its rear edge lie the **original records**: irregular tablets, folded documents, fragments of parchment, sealed bundles, inscribed slips and bound registers, each retaining its own size, material, handwriting, seal and peculiar arrangement. Nothing has been trimmed or rewritten to make these originals resemble one another.

Running forward from each original is a fine bronze guide, thread or ruled line leading to a corresponding place within the ordered register. At the centre of the table, the disparate records have become a sequence of clean rectangular entries of equal external dimensions. These entries align perfectly into rows and columns, permitting immediate comparison. Yet each remains visibly connected to its source and carries a small distinguishing seal, sigil or provenance mark identifying the family from which it came. The visual distinction is critical: **the table is uniform; the evidence is not**.

Some entries should deliberately preserve differences that a careless clerk might have eliminated. One tablet may record an act in one fashion, another by an entirely different formula. One may possess a date but no named witness; another a witness but an uncertain boundary; another may record an increase where a superficially similar entry records an entirely new act. Tabularium does not force them into false equivalence. Their common fields align where comparison is justified, while their peculiarities remain attached in smaller subsidiary tablets or source panels.

His principal gesture should express this relationship. In one hand he holds or steadies an ancient original tablet, visibly irregular and marked by age. With the other he uses a slender bronze stylus to indicate its corresponding entry in the ordered register. His attention is directed not towards the audience but along the line between the two. He is checking that what appears in the table can still be traced without ambiguity to what was actually preserved.

The register must also make **absence** visible. Several compartments remain deliberately empty or carry small dark plaques indicating that nothing has been entered there. These spaces should not resemble unfinished scenery. They are formal parts of the design: declared gaps, unsupported categories and regions for which the surviving material does not justify an entry. Tabularium would rather display a conspicuous vacancy than complete a pleasing pattern with invention. A missing record is itself something that can be accurately recorded.

A second major visual device should represent **reconstruction rather than mere certification**. Near one side of the table stands a compact mechanical frame containing a sequence of movable brass guides. An original tablet may be positioned at its beginning; after passing through a visible series of fixed correspondences, an ordered tablet appears at the other end. Beside the apparatus sits another copy of the same ordered entry, allowing the two to be compared. The symbolism is that an entry is trustworthy not because someone has stamped it trustworthy, but because the route from preserved record to ordered register can be followed again and produces the same result.

No mechanism should suggest alteration of the original. The source tablet enters only by reference: its physical counterpart remains untouched beside the apparatus. The whole composition should repeatedly distinguish **custody**, **interpretation** and **registration** as neighbouring but separate acts.

Behind Tabularium rises the architectural heart of the image: a colossal archive modelled as an impossible enlargement of a Roman record office within the established dark cathedral aesthetic. Floor-to-ceiling walls of black stone contain thousands of precisely numbered niches. Some hold wax tablets, some scrolls, some bound codices, some sealed packets and stone fragments. Long bronze rails divide the archive into ordered sections. The space is immense but not chaotic. Its overwhelming quantity has been subjected to a visible system of location and relation.

At the centre of the rear wall should stand several monumental vertical registers, each composed of aligned stone or bronze panels. Fine lines lead from these panels outward towards the archive niches from which their contents derive. The effect is almost cartographic: the viewer can see that the great summary before them has not replaced the archive behind it. It remains suspended upon it.

Corrections are handled by **succession rather than erasure**. On one side of the chamber, earlier registers remain sealed within numbered recesses. A later corrected register occupies the next recess rather than being written over the earlier one. Bronze plaques may bear Roman numerals or edition marks, with thin connecting lines indicating descent from one version to the next. The older tablets remain intact and consultable. Tabularium's history is cumulative: when understanding changes, the record of the previous understanding survives.

The image may include restrained Latin inscriptions carved into the architecture. Suitable governing phrases would include:

**TABVLARIVM**
**ORDO SINE OBLIVIONE**
*Order without forgetting.*

**FORMA COMMVNIS, TESTIMONIVM PROPRIVM**
*Common form, particular evidence.*

**QVOD SCRIPTUM EST NON MUTATVR VT CONVENIAT**
*What is written is not altered in order to agree.*

**VACVVM QVO NIHIL CONSTAT**
*Empty where nothing is established.*

Across the front edge of the principal table, several large divisions may read:

**FONS** — what was received
**LOCVS** — where it belonged
**ACTVM** — what occurred
**ORDO** — where it stands in relation to the rest
**INTERPRETATIO** — how it was entered
**LACVNAE** — what remains unsupported

These should function as architectural inscriptions rather than explanatory captions.

The lighting should preserve the established visual language: an overwhelmingly dark environment of black stone, aged bronze, parchment and charcoal fabric, illuminated by narrow shafts of cold pale light from high above. Warm gold should appear almost exclusively in Tabularium's eyes, the bronze tracing lines, seals and sparse inscriptions. A stronger shaft should fall onto the centre of the register table, making its ordered rows lucid against the surrounding archive while leaving the original records sufficiently illuminated that their individuality remains visible.

The composition should be broad and symmetrical, with Tabularium occupying approximately the same visual scale as the other canonical Wildcat figures: substantial enough to remain the unmistakable subject, but small enough that the enormous documentary architecture around him communicates the scale of his task. He is not the owner of the records and not their author. He is their registrar.

The essential impression should be that **everything before him has been made comparable, but nothing has been made falsely equivalent**. The viewer should be able to understand the character's principle before reading a single inscription: heterogeneous records remain intact at the edges; precise lines carry them into a common ordered table; every resulting entry remains visibly attached to its origin; gaps remain gaps; earlier editions remain preserved; and Tabularium stands at the centre ensuring that order illuminates the record without ever replacing it.
