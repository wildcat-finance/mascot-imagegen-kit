# Wildcat Lemma

Lemma is the Wildcat as **master of the extracted part**: the figure who takes a large and continuous thing, discovers the divisions already latent within it, and separates it into portions that can stand independently without losing the truth of where they came from. His name reaches back to the Greek *λῆμμα*—that which is taken, received or assumed—and from there to the mathematical lemma: a proposition isolated and established not because it is the final destination of an argument, but because it is a sound piece from which something larger may subsequently be built. Lemma therefore governs the difficult middle state between the whole and its later use. He neither interprets what he receives nor determines what conclusions should be drawn from it. His work is to make the received material discrete, faithful and admissible.

His defining virtue is therefore **faithful separation with provenance**.

He retains the canonical Wildcat form completely: a slender anthropomorphic feline with a sharply faceted pale head, extraordinarily tall triangular ears, broad wedge-shaped skull, angular cheek planes, tapered muzzle, pointed chin, heavy black brows and rectangular golden-yellow eyes. His anatomy is entirely ordinary and coherent—one head, one torso, two arms and two legs. His expression should be concentrated and exact rather than severe: the look of a scholar performing a division whose correctness matters more than its speed. He wears the same restrained dark robes and muted gold fastenings appropriate to the surrounding world, with no ornament that overwhelms the geometric simplicity of the Wildcat himself.

Lemma stands behind a vast black stone table upon which an enormous manuscript has been opened and systematically taken apart. The original work remains plainly visible at one side: broad sheets covered in dense writing, diagrams and headings, some still joined in their original sequence. Across the remainder of the table, individual passages have been lifted cleanly from it and placed into separate narrow frames, tablets or shallow trays. Nothing has been shredded or reduced to meaningless scraps. Each extracted piece is large enough to preserve an intelligible unit—sometimes a paragraph, sometimes an entire headed section, sometimes a compact diagram with its accompanying words. The visual distinction should be between **division** and destruction.

Every separated piece retains an unmistakable connection to its place of origin. Fine golden lines, engraved coordinates, small numbered seals or other archival marks lead back from each extracted fragment to the exact region of the parent manuscript from which it was taken. These connections should be exceptionally orderly. One should be able to look across the table and understand that no fragment has become anonymous merely because it has become independent. Lemma's chamber is a place in which taking something out of context does not mean erasing its context.

A second distinction should be physically visible upon the table. Some fragments contain words copied directly and inviolate from the original; others are assembled presentation pieces in which headings, labels or surrounding structure have been added so that the fragment can stand intelligibly on its own. These two classes must never be allowed to masquerade as one another. Verbatim material might rest beneath clear crystal covers or within severe rectangular black frames, while assembled material carries a subtle secondary border, joining marks or a small gold seal identifying it as constructed. The point is not that one is superior to the other. The point is that **quotation and composition remain distinguishable even when they occupy the same archive**.

Lemma himself is caught in the act of making one such separation. One hand steadies the parent manuscript while the other lifts a single perfectly bounded section from it—not tearing paper, but raising the chosen portion as though the boundaries had always existed and merely required recognition. A narrow beam of pale light may illuminate the lifted fragment, while its golden provenance line remains visibly anchored to the vacant position it occupied within the whole. His attention is directed not towards reading the passage for meaning but towards its edges, heading, extent and attachment to its source.

Around the principal table stand ranks of dark archival cabinets or monumental pigeonholes. Each recess contains one accepted fragment, individually labelled and geometrically aligned. A few rejected pieces remain outside the ordered archive: fragments whose edges overlap improperly, whose origin cannot be established, whose internal structure has been broken, or whose claimed form disagrees with the evidence before him. These are not dramatically destroyed. They simply do not pass inward. Lemma's discipline is expressed through refusal to admit malformed pieces into an otherwise rigorous collection.

The architecture should reinforce the movement from continuity into articulation. Behind Lemma, a single monumental wall or manuscript surface may descend from darkness, dense and almost impossible to comprehend as a whole. Before him, that same abundance has become a field of individually bounded objects arranged in intelligible rows. Golden lines of descent connect the two states. The room should feel less like a library devoted to accumulating books than an ancient **house of propositions**, built to ensure that whatever is taken from a greater body remains recognisably, demonstrably part of that body.

The image should avoid suggesting that Lemma is a judge of truth in the grander sense, an interpreter, or an oracle. He does not decide what the fragments mean and does not assemble them into an answer. His authority ends deliberately at the boundary of preparation. The completed scene should communicate that his achievement is more austere: he has transformed an unwieldy whole into trustworthy pieces, and every piece carries enough evidence of its origin and nature that whoever receives it afterward can know exactly what has been given.

The result should preserve the established visual language: monumental black architecture, restrained pale illumination from above, minute warm-gold mechanisms and inscriptions, deep perspective, obsessive material detail and the solitary pale Wildcat as the principal figure. Lemma should occupy roughly the same visual scale as the other canonical Wildcat figures, but his surroundings should make his particular office immediately legible: **the whole behind him, the extracted parts before him, and an unbroken record of the passage between the two.**
