# Wildcat Janus

Janus is the Wildcat as **keeper of the threshold**: the figure who judges not merely whether a passage may occur, but what may cross with it, what must remain behind, and what must still be true once the traveller has passed through. His name invokes the Roman Janus, god of doors, gates, beginnings, endings and transitions, whose two faces looked simultaneously towards what lay before and what had already been left behind. A doorway, in this older sense, is not empty space between two rooms. It is a place at which the world changes state. Janus exists to ensure that the change is exactly the one permitted.

His defining virtue is therefore **conformance at the moment of transition**. He distrusts the assumption that because an act has an acceptable name, everything occurring during it must also be acceptable. Before anything passes his gate, Janus knows what the traveller may carry, what may be taken, what may be altered, whom they may approach and how much disturbance the passage may impose. Once the crossing is complete, he turns his attention backwards and examines what actually happened. An object missing from one chamber and appearing unexpectedly in another matters. A seal broken without authority matters. A door which technically opened but left its traveller trapped on the far side matters. The permission to cross is not permission to rearrange the house.

He retains the canonical Wildcat form completely: a slender anthropomorphic feline with a sharply faceted pale head, extraordinarily tall triangular ears, broad wedge-shaped skull, angular cheek planes, tapered muzzle, pointed chin, heavy black brows and rectangular golden-yellow eyes. His anatomy remains entirely ordinary and coherent—one head, one torso, two arms and two legs. Janus should not acquire a second literal face. The ancient double aspect is expressed instead through composition: behind him, or within the architecture of the threshold itself, a dark polished surface, relief or precisely aligned reflection reveals the same Wildcat face looking in the contrary direction. The effect should initially evoke the familiar two-faced image of Janus while remaining physically explicable. One living figure looks towards the destination; his reflected counterpart regards the place from which he came.

His expression is severe, attentive and almost impersonal. Unlike a sentinel watching for a particular enemy, Janus watches for **difference**. His eyes should appear to be comparing two conditions rather than staring dramatically at the viewer. His posture may be symmetrical, because symmetry reinforces the idea of a crossing whose two sides can be compared, but he need not stand rigidly. One hand might rest against the jamb of an enormous doorway while the other holds a small plumb, balance, seal or tally whose position can be compared before and after passage. Nothing about him should suggest haste. He controls the instant at which one condition becomes another.

The setting should be a vast, dark, cathedral-like hall constructed almost entirely from **successive thresholds**. Monumental doorframes, arches and porticoes recede into darkness behind him, each marking another boundary between one chamber and the next. Janus stands at the principal gate, roughly the same visual scale as the canonical Wildcat figure, neither dwarfed by the architecture nor enlarged into a giant. The architecture should remain black stone, aged bronze and muted gold, illuminated by narrow shafts of pale light. The principal threshold may divide the image into two related but non-identical regions: the chamber before passage and the chamber after it.

This division should be meticulous rather than fantastical. On corresponding tables to either side of the gate are matched arrangements of weights, sealed vessels, tablets, keys, coins, measures and small mechanical balances. Some objects are permitted to change position across the threshold; others are fixed under seals or within engraved boundaries. Fine golden lines may connect corresponding objects on either side, making visible which things are expected to remain equal and which are allowed to differ. Janus is not trying to make the two chambers identical. He is determining whether their **difference is lawful**.

The doorway itself should carry the strongest symbolic burden. Its lintel and jambs are inscribed with concise restrictions: what may enter, what may leave, what may be touched, and what must remain invariant. These inscriptions should feel like old juridical or sacerdotal law rather than explanation for the viewer. An absent permission is visibly treated as prohibition: objects outside the authorised categories sit behind sealed recesses, untouched. The scene should communicate that Janus recognises no vague licence. What has not been admitted through the gate remains beyond the gate.

Around the principal passage, several smaller trials can embody the different forms by which a crossing may become illegitimate. A balance on one side should correspond exactly to a balance on the other, establishing that substance has not silently disappeared merely because the ceremony appears complete. A narrow return passage should remain visibly unobstructed, showing that entry must never destroy the possibility of departure. A broken vessel or interrupted mechanism might be shown restoring itself to its original arrangement rather than remaining half-changed, expressing the principle that a failed crossing must not leave the world stranded between conditions. An hourglass or descending counterweight may impose a finite allowance upon the passage, implying that a traveller cannot claim unlimited time or effort merely by remaining within the doorway.

Another part of the architecture should suggest the danger of passage turning back upon itself. A subsidiary door may open from within the threshold into an unexpected adjacent chamber, but Janus has marked and chained that route as carefully as the obvious one. His jurisdiction therefore extends not merely to someone retreating through the door they entered, but to attempts to use the crossing as an opportunity to enter some other place entirely. The architecture should resemble a system of doors capable of producing labyrinthine consequences, held intelligible only because Janus treats every possible opening as a distinct boundary.

There should also be a visible distinction between **appearance and consequence**. A traveller's written declaration, token or ceremonial seal may sit upon one side of the gate, but Janus's attention is directed towards the actual disposition of the objects beyond it. He does not accept that because the proper words were spoken, the proper result necessarily followed. Scales, positions, seals and material correspondences provide the evidence. The image should make clear that he judges the transformed world, not the traveller's account of having transformed it correctly.

The ancient association with beginnings and endings should remain present throughout. Janus does not merely guard entrances. He possesses both ends of an action. The forward-looking aspect asks what is about to be permitted; the backward-looking aspect asks what has in fact occurred. Neither judgement is complete without the other. This is why the reflected second face should be important but restrained: not a supernatural apparition competing for attention, but the visual statement that every passage is examined from both temporal directions.

The scene may incorporate **seven successive gates or seven engraved threshold marks**, each corresponding to a progressively deeper test of passage, without explaining them explicitly. They should read as architectural law: seven rings around the principal doorway, seven seals set into the floor, seven lintels receding behind Janus, or seven bronze markers embedded along the crossing. Their repetition should suggest that merely clearing the outermost gate proves nothing about the next. Every threshold has its own jurisdiction.

Most importantly, Janus is not the guardian who prevents change. He is the guardian who makes **bounded change possible**. The chambers on either side of him should not be frozen copies. Something meaningful has genuinely happened between them. Certain weights have moved, a sealed vessel has legitimately changed hands, a door once closed now stands open. Yet everything outside the permitted transformation remains where it belongs. The image succeeds when the viewer understands that Janus's purpose is neither obstruction nor preservation for its own sake, but the precise determination of what a passage is allowed to mean.

The overall composition should retain the established visual language: monumental black architecture, pale stone-like Wildcat anatomy, restrained aged-gold ornament, narrow heavenly illumination, deep perspective, fine material detail and an atmosphere somewhere between archive, tribunal and temple. Janus should appear not as a warrior at a gate but as the **authority by which the gate has meaning at all**—standing exactly where one condition becomes another, looking both ways, permitting the transformation while refusing every consequence that was never part of the bargain.
