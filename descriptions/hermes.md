## Wildcat Hermes

Hermes is the Wildcat as **master of efficient passage**: the figure who asks how much less may be spent in reaching a destination while refusing to permit the destination itself to move. His governing principle is not speed in isolation, nor economy purchased at the cost of hidden change. A proposed improvement is admissible only when it demonstrably consumes less and everything that ought to remain invariant survives the journey untouched. He therefore proceeds one alteration at a time, measuring its advantage and abandoning it completely if any required condition fails.

The name invokes the Greek Hermes, god of travellers, messengers, exchange, crossings and boundaries. His roads were marked by *hermai*, stone pillars standing at thresholds and junctions: fixed points against which movement could be understood. That inheritance should govern the image. Hermes is swift, but his swiftness is disciplined by landmarks. He may discover the shorter road, the lighter burden or the more economical motion, but every boundary stone must still be where it was when he began.

He retains the canonical Wildcat form completely: a lean adult anthropomorphic feline with a sharply faceted pale head, extraordinarily tall narrow triangular ears, broad wedge-shaped skull, angular cheek planes, tapered muzzle, pointed chin, heavy dark brows and rectangular golden-yellow eyes. His anatomy remains entirely ordinary and coherent—one head, one torso, two arms and two legs—and the geometric construction of the face must remain unmistakable.

Hermes stands within an immense subterranean hall of roads and measures. Long parallel causeways of black stone begin near the foreground and disappear through successive arches into darkness. Some routes climb stairs, double around columns or cross narrow bridges; others reach the same distant doorway by startlingly spare lines. At intervals stand severe stone waymarkers, bronze boundary posts and monumental balances. The architecture resembles an ancient customs house, caravanserai, treasury and temple of weights compressed into something cathedral-sized. Nothing suggests frantic motion. The entire chamber exists to make movement measurable.

At the centre is a large square examination platform upon which two journeys can be compared. One route represents the established passage and remains physically intact. Beside it, a second begins identically but contains a single deliberate alteration: one unnecessary turn removed, one burden redistributed, one sequence shortened, one cumbersome fastening replaced by a simpler arrangement. No two alterations are tested together. Around the platform lie other possible refinements still sealed and untouched, making it clear that Hermes permits himself only one changed proposition at a time.

The evidence of expenditure is relentlessly physical. Identical vessels stand at the beginning of both routes, filled with measured quantities of oil, sand, grain or water. Graduated bronze basins at the far end show what remains after each passage. Small balance scales compare the burdens carried. Clepsydrae record elapsed time. Tallies cut into ivory tablets record repeated traversals. Hermes does not infer that the shorter-looking road is cheaper. The saving must appear in the measures themselves.

Yet economy alone is insufficient. Between departure and arrival, each route passes through a succession of massive stone thresholds. At one, a seal must remain unbroken. At another, the carried vessel must weigh exactly what it weighed before. At another, a set of fitted objects must still occupy precisely the same recesses. Another admits the traveller only if the same doors can still be opened with the same keys. A shortcut that reaches the destination cheaply but alters any protected thing is blocked by the architecture itself. Its final gate remains shut.

Several rejected routes should remain visible in the surrounding darkness. One is beautifully short but ends with a cracked vessel. Another saves effort while arriving with a missing weight. Another passes the obvious trials repeatedly yet displaces one small fitted stone from an ordered wall. A particularly seductive path may leap across several turns through an unguarded opening, only to be stopped before the final chamber because nobody can establish what that leap might disturb. These failures are not dramatic disasters. They are simply disqualified.

Repetition matters. Far behind Hermes, the same route can be seen traversed again under slightly different circumstances: lamps burning at different strengths, weights beginning in different arrangements, shutters open or closed against a changing wind. One exact reconstruction demonstrates that the claimed saving can be reproduced; further trials establish that it was not merely an accident of one favourable journey. Hermes distrusts an improvement that succeeds only once.

His clothing should carry the memory of the ancient messenger without turning him into a literal Olympian. A short charcoal travelling mantle falls from the shoulders in a few sharp planes over a close-fitting bone-grey tunic, leaving the arms largely free. Fine antique-gold borders trace its edges. A compact satchel hangs at the waist for seals, weights and measuring instruments. Sandals or narrow fitted boots may contain subtle wing-like geometry in their straps or fastenings, but there should be no actual wings, helmet, caduceus or theatrical divine regalia. The visual language is one of mobility stripped of ornament.

Hermes's principal gesture is small. One hand holds a tiny calibrated weight between finger and thumb, comparing it against its counterpart on a precision balance. The other rests upon a single movable stone in the candidate route, as though deciding whether this one alteration has earned the right to remain. His body is poised rather than static—weight slightly forward, mantle settling behind him—but there is no racing pose. The impression should be of someone capable of enormous speed who considers measurement more important than displaying it.

His expression is alert, penetrating and difficult to satisfy. The golden eyes move between the two routes rather than meeting the viewer, one following the claimed saving and the other watching for what it might have disturbed. The brows are sharply lowered in concentration. There may be the faintest suggestion of cunning at the mouth—not deception, but the pleasure of discovering that an apparently necessary expenditure was never necessary at all.

Gold should function as the visual language of measurement rather than luxury. Bronze-gold lines mark standard lengths in the floor. Small calibrated weights, seal rings, plumb lines and boundary fittings catch the light. The Wildcat's yellow eyes remain the strongest warm accent. Everything else is deep black stone, charcoal, desaturated ivory, tarnished metal and sparse pale parchment. A narrow cold shaft of overhead light isolates Hermes and the central comparison platform while the alternative roads vanish into darkness.

The composition should be broad and strongly perspectival, with Hermes roughly the same visual scale as the canonical solitary Wildcat. The immense parallel roads and repeating thresholds draw the eye toward a distant common destination behind him. The crucial visual fact is that the accepted shorter route and the established longer route terminate at exactly the same place. Their endpoints align perfectly. Their seals match. Their burdens arrive intact. Only the expenditure between them differs.

Hermes therefore must not read simply as the Wildcat of speed. He is the Wildcat of **proven economy**. He treats every needless expenditure as a challenge, every shortcut as a claim requiring examination, and every boundary as something that must survive improvement. His defining proposition is severe: **the better road is the one that costs less while changing nothing except the cost of getting there.**
