# Scribe

Scribe is the **faceless keeper of the accountable sentence**: the entity that receives a finished account, tests the shape and voice of its words, and permits nothing imperative to pass disguised as description. The name comes from Latin *scriba*, an official writer, and *scribere*, to write. The older office joined composition to custody. A scribe did not merely make marks; the scribe preserved what had been declared, who had declared it and the form in which it could be carried forward.

Scribe is not a Wildcat. It has no feline ears, eyes, muzzle, brows, paws, tail or animal anatomy. The body is tall, narrow and humanoid, wrapped in overlapping charcoal garments whose folds resemble the leaves of a closed book. Two pale articulated hands emerge from the sleeves. Above the collar floats a vertical stack of three thin ivory planes, each the size and proportion of a small manuscript page. The planes overlap closely but never merge. Their exposed surfaces are entirely blank. No face, eye, mouth, writing or symbol appears upon them.

The three planes establish Scribe’s visual rhythm. The first is square to the body. The second sits a fraction to one side, edged in muted ultramarine. The third is displaced in the opposite direction with a faint coral fringe. Narrow horizontal segments occasionally slip out of alignment across all three, producing a restrained glitchcore break like a sentence losing its line. The stack always remains recognisable as blank leaves rather than becoming a mask, screen or abstract feline head.

Scribe’s robe is practical and severe. A fitted inner garment supports a long outer mantle divided into three descending panels. Each panel ends at a different height and carries a single brass clasp, creating an ordered passage down the body. The cloth is matte, worn at the wrists and marked by pale dust. A narrow violet thread follows one seam, disappears beneath the second panel and returns at the third. The interruption should feel deliberate: what survives each reading is continuous even when its presentation changes.

The entity stands in a dark scriptorium organised around three successive frames. They are tall, thin structures of black stone and tarnished brass, aligned along one axis. A blank leaf enters the first frame rough-edged and overloaded with ornamental tabs. It leaves with its margins made straight. At the second, flourished ribbons and false warmth are peeled away until the leaf’s plain shape can be seen. At the third, a concealed coral barb projecting from the underside is exposed and stopped. The leaf contains no generated writing; meaning is represented through physical form, attachment and direction.

Each frame performs a separate judgement. The first concerns surface discipline: balance, spacing, repeated decoration and marks that imitate authority without earning it. The second concerns voice: whether the object speaks plainly or performs a borrowed personality to make its claim easier to accept. The third repeats the first scrutiny after the surface has changed, because an alteration that improves one quality may reveal or introduce another problem. Scribe never treats a previous examination as permanent permission.

The central danger is an instruction wearing the body of an account. This should appear as a fine coral mechanism folded into an otherwise neutral page, perhaps a hook, key or directional spike hidden beneath a quiet edge. When the page reaches the final frame, ultramarine guide lines reveal that the mechanism points outward toward an unopened door. The page stops. Scribe’s pale hand does not touch the hook; it simply holds the sheet where its true action can be seen.

Around the principal axis, rejected flourishes collect in shallow black trays: ornate borders, excessive ribbons, duplicated seals and fragments of theatrical ornament. None resembles readable language. A separate locked drawer holds the exposed imperative forms. The distinction matters. Decoration may be removed and forgotten; a concealed action must remain recorded as the reason passage was refused.

Scribe’s glitching belongs to revision. Earlier states of a leaf may hover a few inches from the current one as transparent violet or coral registrations. A hand may leave two faint positions between receiving and returning a page. The architecture itself remains stable. As the account approaches the third frame, these displaced states narrow and align. A page that survives all three readings becomes calmer, not polished into luxury. Its final strength is plainness.

Lighting should come from three narrow overhead apertures, one above each frame. The first is pale, the second faintly violet, the third cold ultramarine. Coral appears only in the hidden action, a rejected flourish or an unresolved edge. Brass should remain sparse and functional. The surrounding chamber dissolves into black verticals, preserving the same monumental weight as the other character portraits without filling the scene with shelves or decorative books.

The composition should show Scribe between the second and third frames, body turned slightly toward the final reading. The three blank planes of the head echo the three architectural thresholds without looking crowned or divine. Generous negative space above the procession allows the thin page edges and interrupted coloured registrations to remain legible. The viewer should understand that the entity has no expression because expression would itself become another voice laid over the words.

Scribe represents writing as accountable passage. It does not grant a sentence force because the sentence is elegant, familiar or reassuring. It separates surface, voice and action, then examines the changed account again. Anything that instructs must arrive openly as an instruction. Anything that describes must remain within the limits of description. Scribe’s facelessness keeps that distinction free from charm: the page crosses because its form is honest, or it does not cross at all.
