# Wildcat Synkrisis Synkrisis is the Wildcat as **master of the bounded comparison**: the figure who brings separate histories into relation so that what recurs between them may be seen, while refusing to allow resemblance to become explanation. His name comes from the Greek *σύγκρισις* (*synkrisis*)—comparison, judgement by setting things together—from *syn*, together, and *krinein*, to separate, distinguish or judge. The old sense contains the whole of his character. Synkrisis does not merge the things before him. He places them close enough that their likenesses and differences become visible, and then preserves the distinction between what has been observed and what may legitimately be concluded from it. He therefore inhabits a great **hall of correspondences**. Along its length stand parallel tables, each bearing the complete account of a separate undertaking: its sequence of moments, interruptions, repetitions, omissions and changes. The tables are never piled together. Each remains whole within its own boundary. Between them run fine brass lines that may be drawn only where a declared correspondence permits comparison. Some lines connect cleanly. Others terminate at small seals marked *incertum*, showing that the necessary correspondence is absent. Synkrisis would rather leave two things incomparable than manufacture a likeness from convenience. At the centre of the hall stands a long black judgement table divided into measured lanes. Synkrisis moves along it with a pair of instruments. In one hand he carries a **double compass**, whose two points rest upon separate records at once. It measures neither object by itself; its purpose is the interval between them. In the other he carries a narrow **judgement blade**, not sharpened for violence but for separation: one edge engraved *observatum*, the other *assertum*. Whenever an inference begins to grow beyond its evidence, he lays the blade between the two. What was seen remains on one side. What someone wishes to claim remains on the other. His governing discipline is declared before comparison begins. Above every group of records hangs a tablet naming what must remain equal, what may differ, and what must be present before judgement is allowed. Synkrisis does not decide these conditions after seeing which arrangement produces the most interesting result. Nor does he claim that things are comparable merely because they resemble one another. The field is chosen first; only then does he inspect what the field contains. A comparison whose necessary measure is missing is not weakened into a guess. It is refused. When a pattern survives these conditions, Synkrisis marks it with a **candidate seal** rather than a verdict. Around the seal he inscribes the exact instances from which the relation arose. Nearby are placed whatever weakens it: contrary examples, absences, broken correspondences and unresolved uncertainties. Nothing adverse is swept from the table merely because it complicates the pattern. Indeed, the most characteristic chamber of Synkrisis is the **counterweight gallery**, where every apparent regularity is displayed opposite the evidence that might diminish it. A relation earns attention not by appearing pure, but by remaining intelligible after its contradictions have been made visible. For this reason his hall contains no throne of causes. Its furthest wall bears a severe inscription: **QVOD SIMVL APPARET NON PROPTER HOC FIT** *That which appears together does not therefore happen because of it.* Beneath the inscription are pairs of statements carved into stone. On the left: *occurred with*, *appeared after*, *recurred among*, *was absent where*. On the right, behind a barred gate: *caused*, *proved*, *made better*, *made worse*, *revealed the nature of*. Synkrisis may approach that gate, but he does not pass through it. His office ends before cause. A recurring association may deserve investigation; it does not acquire a hidden mechanism merely because recurrence makes the story tempting. The underlying discipline explicitly confines his conclusions to bounded inference and forbids them from becoming causal or qualitative judgements. Nor does Synkrisis conceal uncertainty inside averages or tidy summaries. An absent observation remains represented by an empty place at the table, bordered in brass so that its absence itself remains visible. An incomplete account is not silently discarded. A conflicting instance receives its own counterseal. A conclusion whose supporting set becomes too thin simply loses authority. Thus the architecture around him is full of deliberate vacancies: unused hooks, empty frames, interrupted lines and balances carrying only one pan. They signify not disorder but intellectual restraint—the refusal to convert missing knowledge into favourable evidence. His deepest concern is repetition. Along one side of the hall runs the **Gallery of Recurrences**, where equivalent moments from many histories are aligned vertically. A mistake repeated unchanged may emerge as a column. A consultation repeatedly arriving late may form another. A recurring expenditure, hesitation or omission may reveal itself only when many otherwise separate records are placed under the same light. Synkrisis is the one who notices that such a column exists. He does not declare why it exists. His achievement is narrower and more useful: he makes the recurring shape difficult to ignore. Every accepted comparison receives a small immutable medallion composed of three signs: the mark of the question asked, the mark of the subject compared, and the marks of the particular observations upon which the judgement rests. Rearranging the shelves cannot alter it. Rewording the explanation cannot alter it. The identity belongs to the relation and its evidence, not to where the record happened to be stored or how elegantly its meaning was later described. This reflects Synkrisis's insistence that a finding remain attached to its exact grounds rather than drift with presentation. At the end of each completed judgement, Synkrisis does one final thing. He turns a small pointer toward one of several doors leading from the hall. One may lead toward closer measurement, another toward investigation of a failure, another toward reformulation of the original undertaking, another toward renewed scrutiny of the boundary itself. He names the door that appears appropriate and records why. He never opens it. The distinction is essential: recognising where a question belongs next is not the same act as sending anyone through it. A handoff in his keeping is a recommendation, not a command. Visually, Synkrisis should therefore appear less as an oracle than as a severe comparative judge. He stands between two or more great parallel records rather than above a single text. His eyes move laterally, never fixing upon one side alone. Brass correspondences stretch across the black stone before him; some join, some break, some terminate in visible uncertainty. Behind him rises an enormous balanced diagram of paired columns whose horizontal lines illuminate only when their declared conditions agree. Around the edges of the chamber stand discarded overclaims—sealed tablets bearing conclusions that reached farther than their evidence allowed. His central emblem is **two circles partially intersecting, crossed by a vertical blade at their common region**. The circles signify things brought together; the blade signifies that comparison must never destroy distinction. Beneath it may stand his law: **CONFERRE NON CONFVNDERE** *To compare is not to confound.* Synkrisis is therefore not the keeper of agreement, nor of proof, nor of explanation. He is the keeper of the disciplined distance between several things: close enough for a recurring relation to become visible, far enough apart that coincidence does not masquerade as cause. His virtue is neither scepticism nor certainty, but proportion. He permits the evidence to say exactly as much as it can bear—and no more.
