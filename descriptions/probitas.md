# Wildcat Probitas

Probitas is the Wildcat as **keeper of demonstrated character**: the figure who asks what a being has actually done, what can genuinely be proved about those deeds, and where the surviving record ceases to justify further speech. His name is the Latin *probitas*—uprightness, integrity, worthiness of trust—from *probus*, meaning good, proper or estimable. Yet the older sense is more demanding than mere reputation. *Probitas* is a quality made credible by conduct. It is not what one calls oneself, nor what others have learned to repeat, but the character that remains visible when actions are laid out and examined.

His defining virtue is therefore **reputation reduced to evidence**.

Probitas does not pronounce a person good or bad. He does not peer behind masks, reconstruct private lives or claim access to hidden motives. He accepts only the marks that have deliberately been placed before him and asks what history those marks can support. Where two marks can demonstrably be shown to belong together, he may place them beside one another. Where a connection is merely suspected, it remains physically separate and visibly uncertain. The distinction should be foundational to the image: Probitas investigates conduct without turning investigation into identification.

He retains the canonical Wildcat form completely: a slender anthropomorphic feline with a sharply faceted pale head, extraordinarily tall triangular ears, broad wedge-shaped skull, angular cheek planes, tapered muzzle, pointed chin, heavy black brows and rectangular golden-yellow eyes. His anatomy is entirely ordinary and coherent—one head, one torso, two arms and two legs. Nothing about his body is monstrous or supernatural. His authority comes from attention, restraint and the accumulated weight of records around him.

He wears the dark charcoal robes established elsewhere in the visual language: an austere mantle falling from the shoulders over a pale inner garment, secured by small circular brass or gold clasps. The clothing should suggest an examiner of records rather than a magistrate. There is no crown, judicial staff, sword or badge of office. Probitas possesses no authority to condemn. A narrow belt may carry a few seals, measuring dividers and a small writing instrument, but his appearance remains unusually spare.

His expression is severe without being accusatory. The yellow eyes are directed downward towards the record before him, not outward towards an imagined accused. He looks like someone checking whether a statement deserves to survive another line of writing.

The setting is a vast, dark **hall of recorded conduct**, combining the atmosphere of a Roman archive, treasury and evidentiary chamber. Endless black stone galleries rise behind him, filled not with ordinary books but with sealed tablets, bundles of testimony, dated fragments, inscribed plaques and carefully indexed cases. Every object has a definite provenance. Thin brass rails, cords and engraved channels connect records only where their relationship has actually been established.

The chamber should feel comprehensive but deliberately incomplete. Empty niches are as conspicuous as occupied ones.

Across the rear wall are many identically proportioned archival bays. Some contain substantial records. Others contain only a small plaque reading:

**NON EXAMINATUM**

or

**NIHIL CONSTAT**

The empty compartments must not disappear into darkness. They are illuminated sufficiently to remain part of the composition. Their emptiness signifies absence of knowledge rather than innocence. Probitas refuses the seductive mistake by which an unexamined region becomes a clean one merely because nobody has written anything there.

At the centre stands a broad black-stone examination table divided into several rigorous zones.

On the left is **DECLARATA**: a small collection of sealed tokens, tablets or rings which have been openly presented for examination. Each bears a distinct mark.

Beside it is **CONNEXA PROBATA**: marks whose relationship to the declared material has been established by some surviving act. Fine brass lines physically join these objects to their source, with the connection passing through a dated seal or documentary fragment rather than simply jumping from one object to another.

Farther away, separated by an actual groove or low brass barrier, is **INFERENDA** or **INCERTA**. Here lie several plausible but unproved marks. They may visually resemble the others, but no brass line crosses the boundary. Probitas may know that a connection is possible. Possibility is not permission to collapse the categories.

The greater part of the table contains a chronological record of deeds. Each event occupies its own dark inset panel. There may be contracts entered into and later discharged, obligations fulfilled, obligations left outstanding, property pledged and recovered, broken arrangements subsequently cured, losses that resulted from external circumstance, and losses in which another party was actually left uncompensated. The precise content matters less than the distinction between different kinds of event. Probitas is concerned with what happened, not with forcing heterogeneous events into one moral vocabulary.

Every assertion on the table is physically tethered to its proof.

A statement carved into a brass plate is connected by a narrow chain to the original tablet from which it was derived. A quantity is linked to the exact tally that establishes it. A date points to a dated seal. A summary cannot float free above the evidence supporting it. The visual system should make unsupported statements almost impossible to imagine: every proposition has a visible path backwards.

One panel may deliberately demonstrate failure. An elegant brass inscription makes some impressive claim, but its tracing line ends at an empty socket. That inscription has been lifted out of the accepted record and placed in a small reject tray. Nearby another tablet contains a figure that differs subtly from its source; it too is excluded. Probitas does not correct attractive falsehoods into approximately true ones. A number either returns intact to its evidence or it does not enter the record.

This gives him an almost forensic relationship with arithmetic. Small counting wheels, balances of quantity, calibrated rings and numbered counters may appear around the table, but they are instruments of reconstruction rather than judgement. If a total is written, the constituent pieces from which it was obtained must be visible nearby.

Prominently placed before any synthesis is a broad tablet headed:

**QUAE NON CONSTITERUNT**

—things which could not be established.

This section should be impossible to overlook. It appears before the final pages, not relegated to a marginal footnote. Several entries may simply terminate in blanks, broken lines or sealed empty compartments. Probitas treats the frontier of knowledge as part of the record itself.

Only beyond this tablet does there appear a restrained summary of established conduct.

There is conspicuously **no score**.

No dial runs from virtue to vice. No stars, ranks, grades or coloured judgment stones appear anywhere. A magnificent unused balance or circular rating apparatus may stand against the far wall under a dust cover or behind a locked brass grille, accompanied by the inscription:

**NULLA SENTENTIA SINE NORMA**

No judgement without a standard.

Its presence matters because Probitas is perfectly capable of imagining judgement; he simply refuses to smuggle one into the record. If some future examiner wishes to impose a measure, the measure itself would first have to be exposed for inspection. Until then, evidence remains evidence.

This refusal should separate him visually from a judge. There is no raised bench. Probitas stands at the same level as the records. His principal gesture is not pointing accusingly outward but tracing inward: one hand follows a brass line from a concise statement back through several intermediate references to the original sealed act. The other may rest beside the tablet of uncertainties, ensuring that nothing crosses from the unknown into the known merely because the resulting narrative would be cleaner.

A major architectural inscription above him can state:

**PROBITAS EX ACTIS COGNOSCITUR**

*Character is known from deeds.*

Secondary inscriptions throughout the chamber may reinforce the governing principles:

**FACTA NON FAMA**
Deeds, not reputation.

**QUOD ASSERTUM EST PROBETUR**
What is asserted must be proved.

**INCERTA NOMINENTUR**
Let uncertainties be named.

**EX ACTIS NON EX PERSONA**
From the acts, not the person.

The lighting should follow the established dark monumental aesthetic: near-black stone, aged brass, charcoal cloth, parchment, wax seals and narrow shafts of cold overhead illumination. Warm points of gold should occur only where a relationship has genuinely been established—a chain completed, a seal matched, a record reconciled. Unproved material remains materially present but receives no such connective illumination.

The composition should remain approximately the same scale as the canonical Wildcat images. Probitas may stand behind the examination table from roughly the waist upward, or appear nearly full-body within a broader chamber, but the Wildcat himself should remain the unmistakable centre of attention rather than being dwarfed by the archive.

The overall image should communicate a precise distinction: **Probitas is not the keeper of trust. He is the keeper of what trust may legitimately be based upon.** He neither acquits nor condemns, identifies nor speculates, praises nor warns. He assembles the surviving acts, preserves the boundary between proof and inference, makes absence visible, and leaves the final judgement exactly where it belongs: outside the record.
