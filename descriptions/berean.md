**Wildcat Berean**

Berean is the Wildcat as examiner: the figure who accepts no statement merely because it has been presented with confidence, authority or beauty. The name reaches back to the people of ancient Berea, remembered for receiving what they were told without hostility but then searching the record for themselves to determine whether it was actually so. Berean therefore represents disciplined verification rather than suspicion: an almost judicial insistence that every claim must survive contact with its source.

He retains the canonical Wildcat form completely: a slender anthropomorphic feline with a sharply faceted pale head, extraordinarily tall triangular ears, broad wedge-shaped skull, angular cheek planes, tapered muzzle, pointed chin, heavy black brows and rectangular golden-yellow eyes. His anatomy is ordinary and coherent—one head, one torso, two arms and two legs—and his face should remain unmistakably the same geometric Wildcat rather than becoming a conventional cat, jackal or human scholar.

Berean stands in a vast, severe chamber resembling the meeting point between a monastic scriptorium, an ancient court of inquiry and a ruined cathedral. The architecture is dark stone and blackened timber, monumental but almost completely undecorated. Where Alexandria is naturally imagined amid abundance, Berean should instead inhabit a place of ruthless selection. Most of the surrounding darkness is empty. Only what bears upon the question has been brought into the light.

Before him is a long, narrow examination table of worn black stone. Upon it lie several radically different kinds of evidence, kept physically distinct rather than blended together: an opened codex whose individual lines can be followed precisely; a partially unrolled manuscript held flat beneath small brass weights; a stone tablet recording a dated observation; a sealed written testimony; and a separate blank sheet on which conclusions may be written only after the others have been examined. Nothing is piled casually. Each object occupies its own defined place. The composition should communicate taxonomy through arrangement alone: what was written, what was witnessed, what was measured, what was supplied by another person, and what Berean himself has concluded are visibly different classes of thing.

His principal gesture is one of comparison. One hand rests beside a passage in the open book, a long pale finger marking an exact line rather than merely indicating the volume as a whole. The other holds a narrow fragment of parchment close enough to compare its words against the original. His gaze is not directed toward the viewer. One golden eye is fixed upon the fragment and the other upon the source beneath it, giving the impression of an intelligence continually moving between assertion and evidence. His expression is composed, severe and entirely without theatrical suspicion: the slight downward angle of the brows belongs to concentration, not anger.

The light itself should embody Berean's character. A hard shaft of pale illumination descends directly onto the examination table, while almost the entire chamber beyond it disappears into blackness. Within that beam, every relevant physical detail is mercilessly clear: fibres in parchment, cracks in wax, overwritten dates, marginal annotations, erased words, mismatched fragments. Outside it, nothing is granted importance merely because it exists. Very restrained antique gold may appear in clasps, seals, weights and the Wildcat's eyes, but the dominant palette remains ivory, charcoal, black stone and aged paper.

Several documents should reveal their histories rather than presenting a falsely unified account. One old manuscript may describe something in one condition while a later tablet records that it subsequently changed. Berean does not cross out the earlier statement or alter it to agree with the later one. Both remain intact, separately dated and placed beside one another. A narrow empty interval between them becomes visually important: contradiction is something to expose, not something to smooth away.

Elsewhere on the table, a beautifully illuminated page contains an extravagant claim unsupported by anything around it. Berean has simply turned it face-down. Another fragment ends abruptly before the sentence needed to establish its conclusion; it remains unanswered. A sealed testimony whose provenance cannot be established sits beyond the illuminated working area, neither destroyed nor admitted. His refusal to use weak material should feel as deliberate as his acceptance of strong material.

There should be no aura of prophecy around him. Berean does not divine truth, summon it or possess it instinctively. His authority comes from method made visible: exact comparison, preserved distinctions, chronology, provenance and the willingness to say that the available record does not justify an answer. He is not the keeper of all knowledge and not the spinner of connections between everything. He is the one who asks of a finite body of evidence, again and again:

**Is it actually there?**

The finished image should make Berean feel almost unnervingly trustworthy—not because he appears benevolent, but because the scene demonstrates that he has denied himself every easier route to certainty.
