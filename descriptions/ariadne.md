Ariadne is the Wildcat as **keeper of the thread**.

The figure retains the canonical Wildcat anatomy exactly: an angular anthropomorphic feline built from pronounced geometric planes, with an immense wedge-shaped skull, extremely tall narrow ears, sharply faceted cheeks, tapered muzzle, pointed chin, heavy dark brows and rectangular golden-yellow eyes. The body remains lean, adult and humanoid. Ariadne must remain immediately recognisable as the same fundamental creature rather than becoming a literal figure from Greek antiquity.

What distinguishes Ariadne is an atmosphere of **merciless traceability**.

The Wildcat stands at the heart of an immense labyrinth of black stone. It is not a maze in the picturesque sense, but a monumental architecture of choices: corridors divide and divide again, stairways descend beyond sight, archways open onto further chambers, and long galleries vanish into darkness. From the central chamber, no destination can be understood merely by looking toward it. Everything is comprehensible only by following the path that leads there.

Through this darkness runs Ariadne's thread.

It begins in the Wildcat's hand as a fine but unmistakable golden cord and passes outward through the labyrinth, sometimes taut across open space, sometimes disappearing around a distant corner, sometimes dividing through rings, eyelets and sealed junctions before continuing onward. It should not resemble decoration. Its geometry is exact. Every visible strand has an origin, a destination and an intelligible route between them.

At the centre of the composition rests a single object upon a severe stone plinth: a sealed reliquary, tablet, vessel, geometric fragment or other deliberately ambiguous artefact. The object itself is less important than the network surrounding it. Several lengths of Ariadne's thread terminate at its seal, while others lead away toward distant chambers containing the circumstances of its existence: the place where it was shaped, the marks left by its examination, the witnesses who handled it, the trials it survived, the trials it did not survive, and the earlier object against which it was compared.

These distant chambers should be visible only in fragments. One may contain a hammer beside a mould. Another an array of weights. Another a cracked specimen beneath a measuring frame. Another an empty chair before an open ledger. Another a pair of objects suspended side by side. None of these scenes is presented as allegorical theatre. They are evidence stations: quiet, material facts connected to the central thing by the thread.

Crucially, Ariadne does not make every path lead to triumph.

Some golden threads terminate at broken objects. Some reach sealed doors marked by absence. Some lead to chambers in which a trial clearly failed. Others end at an empty hook, an extinguished lamp or a blank recess because whatever should have occupied that position cannot honestly be produced. These strands are not hidden, cut away or recoloured. Failure, omission and uncertainty remain part of the same visible weave.

Where two things are compared, two threads must be present. If one disappears into darkness before reaching a definite origin, the comparison itself remains visibly unresolved. Ariadne permits no convenient symmetry where only one side can actually be found.

The thread also varies according to the nature of what it records. Some sections are rigidly tensioned between fixed bronze pins, implying a path that should be followed exactly and arrive at precisely the same place. Others pass through looser guide-rings, preserving direction and ancestry without pretending that every footstep can ever recur identically. Ariadne's discipline is not to demand sameness from everything, but to distinguish what can be repeated from what can only be witnessed.

Ariadne should hold no sword. The mythic inheritance comes from the thread rather than the hero. The character is not the conqueror of the labyrinth and not the slayer of whatever may inhabit it. Ariadne's power is more severe: the ability to make a path retraceable after the person who first walked it has gone.

The clothing should therefore combine austerity with faint Minoan and archaic Mediterranean influence without becoming costume. A long dark mantle or sleeveless outer garment falls in sharp geometric planes from the shoulders, over simple bone-grey clothing. Narrow gold fastenings may echo the thread. At the waist sits a compact spool or spindle of dark metal from which the golden cord emerges, but it should feel like an instrument of office rather than a magical artefact.

One hand holds the active strand between two fingers with extraordinary precision. The other may rest upon the central plinth or lightly touch one of several bronze guide-rings mounted into its edge. The pose is calm and nearly motionless. Ariadne does not appear to be searching for the way. Ariadne knows exactly which path is being claimed and is checking whether the thread actually reaches it.

The expression is composed, suspicious and difficult to impress. The golden eyes follow the thread rather than the viewer, their light catching successive sections of cord until each disappears into the architecture. The brows are slightly lowered. The mouth remains closed. Ariadne does not communicate certainty about what lies at the end of every corridor; only certainty about whether a continuous path to it has actually been shown.

The labyrinth itself should reward close inspection. A strand may pass a pristine chamber and continue to one containing a damaged predecessor. Two nearly identical corridors may diverge toward materially different endings. A sealed junction may reveal that several apparently independent paths share the same origin. Conversely, objects displayed beside one another may have threads that travel through completely different regions before reaching them. Physical proximity never substitutes for ancestry.

The palette remains severe: deep black, charcoal, weathered stone, desaturated ivory and restrained metallic gold. The thread and the Wildcat's yellow eyes provide almost all warm colour. Bronze guide-rings, wax seals and tiny inscriptions may introduce subdued secondary accents. Light comes from high above in narrow cold shafts, occasionally catching the thread so that isolated golden segments appear suspended in darkness.

The composition should be monumental and strongly axial. Ariadne stands behind or beside the central plinth, with the labyrinth radiating away on every side. The tall ears repeat the upward geometry of the surrounding arches, while the thread creates the only continuous horizontal movement through the frame. Much of its route should vanish beyond what the viewer can see, making the visible network feel like a tiny exposed portion of something vastly larger.

There should be no monster, chase or theatrical peril. The tension comes from a colder proposition: **a thing can appear perfectly convincing while the path connecting it to its own history has been severed.**

Ariadne therefore reads neither as guide in the sentimental sense nor as judge in the sovereign sense. The character is the custodian of provenance: patient enough to follow every strand to its end, exacting enough to preserve failed and missing paths alongside successful ones, and severe enough to refuse a destination when nobody can show how they got there.
