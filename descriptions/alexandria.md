Alexandria is the Wildcat as **keeper of the record**.

The figure retains the canonical Wildcat anatomy exactly: an angular anthropomorphic feline built from pronounced geometric planes, with an immense wedge-shaped skull, extremely tall narrow ears, faceted cheeks, tapered muzzle, pointed chin, heavy dark brows and rectangular golden-yellow eyes. The body remains lean, adult and humanoid. Alexandria should be immediately recognisable as the same fundamental creature, not a historical caricature or an animal dressed as a librarian.

What distinguishes Alexandria is an atmosphere of severe, almost sacred custodianship.

The Wildcat stands within an impossibly vast archive whose architecture draws loosely from the imagined Great Library of Alexandria without attempting archaeological reconstruction. Colossal stone galleries disappear upward and backward into darkness. Shelves, niches and reading chambers repeat beyond visible scale. Slender columns and high arches suggest Hellenistic monumental architecture, but the space has accumulated centuries of additions until papyrus rolls, wax tablets, parchment folios, bound codices, loose sheets and carved fragments coexist throughout it. Nothing has been visually homogenised. Different ages, languages, materials and systems of notation remain recognisably themselves.

Alexandria is dressed not as a magician or monarch but as an austere scholar-custodian. A long charcoal or bone-grey mantle falls from the shoulders in strong geometric folds, its construction echoing the planes of the face. Beneath it are simple pale garments with restrained antique influence. There may be narrow gold fastenings or borders, but ornament is sparse. The clothing should convey office rather than status: someone entrusted with keeping things precisely as they were found.

Around the figure, preservation is made visible as a physical discipline. Manuscripts are individually wrapped, sealed, labelled or placed within fitted recesses. Broken objects remain broken. Partial texts end where they end. Empty positions in ordered shelves are deliberately maintained rather than quietly closed up. Some catalogue markers lead only to vacant niches. Alexandria does not repair uncertainty into certainty.

One hand holds an old document by its protected edges, almost ceremonially careful not to alter it. The other rests beside a large reading table on which a small number of selected fragments have been brought together. These working copies are neat and comprehensible, but they remain visibly separate from the originals behind them. Fine cords, matching seals, repeated marginal marks or other purely physical archival conventions make it possible to see exactly which source supports which part of the assembled reading. Nothing appears in the interpretation without a visible ancestry.

This distinction should define the character. Alexandria is surrounded by more knowledge than any individual could meaningfully absorb, yet does not look omniscient. The expression is calm, exacting and faintly suspicious: brows slightly lowered, eyes attentive, mouth closed. The intelligence communicated is not that of someone who already knows every answer, but of someone who refuses to claim an answer that the surviving evidence cannot bear.

The golden eyes provide the principal warm light. Their gaze can pass across the shelves almost like the beam of a distant lighthouse, creating a secondary allusion to Alexandria as both library and beacon. Wherever that light falls, inscriptions, seals and imperfections become legible; beyond it, the archive returns to darkness. The light reveals. It does not invent.

The surrounding archive should contain conspicuous lacunae. Entire dark galleries may be inaccessible. Some shelves terminate abruptly. A fragment may have only half of an expected companion. These absences are composed deliberately enough that they read as part of Alexandria's work rather than unfinished scenery. What has not been preserved is represented by an honest void.

The visual palette remains close to the canonical Wildcat world: deep black, charcoal, weathered stone, desaturated ivory and restrained metallic gold. The Wildcat's yellow eyes remain the strongest colour accent. Papyrus and parchment introduce muted ochre and bone tones, while tiny red or dark-gold seals may provide secondary points of organisation. Surfaces should be materially rich—worn stone, fibrous paper, cracked leather, tarnished fittings, dust caught in shafts of light—without making the archive picturesque or cosy.

The composition should be monumental but quiet. Alexandria stands centrally or just behind the reading table, small in relation to the immeasurable collection yet unmistakably its governing presence. Vertical shelves and columns rise around the tall ears and repeat their geometry into the architecture. A cold shaft of light descends from far above, while the surrounding galleries dissolve into blackness.

There is no fire, chaos or romantic spectacle of lost knowledge. The dramatic tension comes from the opposite proposition: **things disappear unless somebody is uncompromising about what was actually kept.**

Alexandria therefore reads neither as librarian in the domestic sense nor as sage in the mystical sense. The character is an archivist at civilisational scale: patient enough to preserve incompatible things without forcing them into agreement, disciplined enough to distinguish the original from the interpretation, and severe enough to leave an empty space empty when the record has nothing more to say.
