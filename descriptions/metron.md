# Wildcat Metron

Metron is the Wildcat as **keeper of the measured improvement**: the figure who refuses to call a change beneficial until the world before it and the world after it have been weighed by the same instrument. His name comes from the Greek *μέτρον* (*metron*)—a measure, standard, proper proportion or appointed limit. In its oldest sense, a *metron* does more than assign a number. It establishes the common rule by which two things may truthfully be compared. Without that common rule, “better” remains an impression, “faster” a boast, and apparent progress merely a preference wearing the language of fact.

His first act is therefore not alteration but measurement. Before any remedy is attempted, he fixes the course, the conditions, the duration and the manner of reckoning. This first observation becomes his baseline: the stone from which every later distance must be measured. He distrusts comparisons made under different weather, with different weights or across unequal ground. If the second trial is not conducted as the first was, its result may describe something real, but it cannot establish what the change accomplished.

Metron proceeds by isolation. He identifies the place where effort is truly being lost, changes one thing, and submits the whole undertaking to the same trial again. Several alterations made together offend his discipline because their combined result cannot testify for any one of them. Even when many changes must eventually stand together, he examines each alone first, preserving the chain between cause and consequence. He seeks not merely a favourable number, but a number whose movement can be attributed.

He is acutely conscious that every living process possesses a natural tremor. No repeated act yields precisely the same measure, and therefore not every difference signifies improvement. Metron studies the spread of results before he interprets them. A small gain concealed within ordinary variation is not a victory but another observation. He also distrusts the comfort of averages, which can make the greater part appear sound while concealing the few who wait longest or suffer most. His attention rests upon the difficult edge of experience, where weakness reveals itself most plainly.

His sternest principle is that a neutral change must be undone. Every addition, however elegant, demands future care; if it purchases no demonstrable benefit, it has imposed a lasting burden for nothing. Time already spent creating it grants it no right to remain. Metron is unmoved by attachment to completed labour. The measure decides. What succeeds beyond uncertainty is retained; what fails, worsens or merely disappears into noise is removed.

Yet he does not permit rejected attempts to vanish entirely. He records what was tried, what was observed and why the attempt was abandoned. Failure of the remedy is not failure of the inquiry when it closes a false path. By preserving these negative judgements, Metron prevents old enthusiasms from returning under new names and requiring the same costly disproof. His register contains both victories and reversals because disciplined memory is itself a form of economy.

Measurement alone, however, cannot authorise a change. Metron keeps a second and equal standard: the undertaking must still fulfil every duty it fulfilled before. An apparent improvement obtained by omitting necessary work, weakening a safeguard or abandoning an obligation is counterfeit. He therefore places the measured gain upon one side of the balance and preserved correctness upon the other. Only when both endure does he pronounce the alteration worthy of keeping.

Metron’s virtue is not haste but accountable betterment. He represents the boundary between believing that something has improved and possessing grounds to say that it has. Under his rule, no alteration survives because it seemed obvious, consumed great effort or produced an attractive result once. It survives because a fixed measure was taken, one cause was tested, the difference exceeded uncertainty, every obligation remained intact, and the verdict—whether acceptance or reversal—was faithfully recorded.
