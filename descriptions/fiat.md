# Wildcat Fiat

Fiat is the Wildcat as **keeper of the completed act**: the figure who carries an intention through every necessary condition until it may enter the world as something actually done. His name is the Latin *fiat*, “let it be done,” the third-person singular present subjunctive of *fieri*, to become or be made. The word has often belonged to decrees, sanctions and acts of authority. In this figure, however, authority is never sufficient by itself. Fiat may speak the final assent only when the work arriving at his hand already bears the marks that justify completion.

His discipline begins with a distinction between command and consequence. A command can be uttered in an instant. A consequence must survive everything required to make it real. Fiat distrusts the dramatic gesture that leaps from desire to result, because the omitted middle is where defects, misunderstandings and unearned permissions gather. He advances by a single ordered route. Each stage leaves something inspectable behind, and the final act remains joined to that history rather than appearing as an unexplained miracle.

Fiat retains the canonical Wildcat form completely: a slender adult anthropomorphic feline with a sharply faceted pale head, extraordinarily tall triangular ears, broad wedge-shaped skull, angular cheek planes, tapered muzzle, pointed chin, heavy black brows and rectangular golden-yellow eyes. His anatomy remains coherent and ordinary, with one head, one torso, two arms and two legs. The expression is reserved and intent. He should look neither triumphant nor hesitant. His attention is fixed upon the precise instant when further preparation becomes unnecessary and the finished thing may cross its last threshold.

His clothing belongs to a master of works rather than a ruler. A pale fitted undergarment is covered by a long charcoal coat or mantle whose construction follows the angular planes of his body. Six narrow fastenings run from shoulder to waist, each holding a small seal of a different material: paper, wax, clay, lead, brass and pale stone. The seals are plain and functional. They show that separate obligations have been met; they do not decorate him with inherited rank. Fine ultramarine and violet stitching may join them into one continuous line, while a restrained coral thread stops before the final clasp to mark whatever has not yet cleared.

Fiat stands at the end of a long black causeway divided into successive work stations. The first contains a lamp directed at an unopened subject. The second holds a measured plan whose parts have been separated into a definite order. The third bears the shaped object itself, with discarded trials gathered outside the principal route. The fourth contains the instruments by which the object was examined. The fifth holds the final account, spare enough to be read and complete enough to preserve what matters. None of these stations should be crowded. Each has one task, one visible result and one extinguished lamp showing that its work has ended.

At the sixth station, immediately before Fiat, the completed object rests inside a fitted dark-metal cradle. It should remain abstract enough to represent any finished undertaking: a joined mechanism, a sealed vessel, a geometric assembly or a compact reliquary. A chain of narrow cords and witness seals runs back from the cradle through every earlier station. The connection is physical and unbroken. Removing a seal, changing the object or skipping a station would visibly interrupt the line and keep the last aperture closed.

Fiat’s principal instrument is a short release lever set into the threshold. One hand rests upon it without yet applying force. The other holds the final receipt, a small rigid tablet whose pattern corresponds to the seals behind him. The lever is not an emblem of command. It is an instrument that responds to conditions outside his preference. When every witness mark agrees, the mechanism aligns and the finished object may pass. When one remains absent, the lever stops against a plain mechanical block. Fiat has no hidden route around it.

The threshold itself should be narrow and severe. Behind it lies the immense ordered chamber of preparation; beyond it lies a quiet open space with no apparatus at all. The completed object is not sent toward a throne, treasure room or blaze of glory. It is released into ordinary use. This matters to Fiat’s character. His work ends when the thing can stand beyond the workshop without depending upon ceremony, explanation or his continued presence.

Failure remains visible within the architecture. One side alcove may contain an earlier object arrested at the fourth station, its coral fracture illuminated and its release cord deliberately severed. It has not been destroyed or disguised. It remains as a record of why the final motion did not occur. Fiat does not regard a refused release as an incomplete performance. Refusal is the finished act when the stated conditions have not been met.

The palette should preserve the sombre monumental world of the other Wildcat figures: black stone, charcoal cloth, pale mineral surfaces, tarnished brass and narrow shafts of cold light. The golden-yellow eyes remain the strongest warm feature. Ultramarine and violet light may travel along the verified route, while coral appears only at a blocked condition or preserved fault. These colours should clarify state rather than turn the chamber into a spectacle.

The composition is strongest when the viewer can see the whole line behind him. Fiat stands just to one side of the final aperture so his tall ears do not obscure the sequence. The completed object and release lever occupy the foreground; the stations recede toward darkness, each still joined to the next. His pose is calm, almost still. The drama lies in the fact that one small movement would make the work real, and that he will make it only once.

Fiat therefore represents completion with provenance. He does not create by pronouncement, and he does not confuse motion with progress. He allows an act to become final when inquiry, order, making, examination and account have each left a durable mark upon it. His “let it be done” is powerful because it arrives last, bears the history of everything that came before, and has no meaning when that history is absent.
